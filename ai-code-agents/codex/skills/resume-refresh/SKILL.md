# Resume Refresh

Improve the selected resume sections by uncovering concrete contributions, explaining outcomes, and shaping useful interview conversations. Support a partial refresh, a full review, or tailoring to a supplied job description; follow the user's requested scope.

## Monthly performance reviews

Use [references/monthly-performance-review.md](references/monthly-performance-review.md) to prepare for JC's monthly reviews with Derek or capture supplied notes afterward. Track accomplishments, evidence, feedback, and agreed goals over time. For review-only requests, work from the available notes without requiring a resume search.

Keep dated review records in a user-selected document or a saved deliverable, separate from the skill instructions. Reuse continuing accomplishment records across months, and identify newly delivered outcomes. Suggest strong, supported contributions as candidate resume bullets when useful; prepare changes for review before updating the canonical resume or LinkedIn. Meeting plans alone do not request reminders, scheduling, or messages to Derek.

## Establish the working version

- Read [references/user-resume-context.md](references/user-resume-context.md) when locating JC's existing resumes. These are dated pointers, not proof of the current version.
- Inspect the selected document and relevant local PDFs. Match content, especially recent roles and accomplishments, rather than relying on filenames or modification times alone.
- If the user supplies a target job description, use it to choose relevant evidence and vocabulary. For a general refresh, proceed without requiring a job posting.
- Preserve naming variants of the same current resume. Distinguish an older version from a current resume tailored to another role. Do not archive, rename, upload, or change sharing as an automatic part of a refresh.

## Discover the evidence before rewriting

Identify weak or missing accomplishments in the selected sections. Ask only the questions that materially improve them, in small batches. Reuse answers and sources already available in the conversation.

Useful questions:

- What problem existed, who experienced it, and what changed after your work?
- What did you personally own, and what did the team deliver?
- What was the scale: users, orders, locations, systems, manual steps, or data volume?
- How do you know it worked: before/after measurements, usage, production behavior, customer feedback, or a manager's assessment?
- What difficult engineering decision, migration, integration, or tradeoff could you explain confidently in an interview?

Distinguish measured results, documented scope, defensible estimates, targets, and unknowns. Keep targets labeled as targets; do not turn projected savings or adoption into achieved results. Never invent numbers, business wins, titles, ownership, or shipped status. A shipped workflow or eliminated manual process can be a meaningful outcome without a percentage. Use estimates only when the user supplies a reasonable basis, and label them clearly.

## Write and order the accomplishments

- Give each bullet a concrete contribution and an outcome. A useful shape is action + problem or scope + result, with relevant technical detail when it helps establish ability.
- Replace generic duties such as fixing bugs, maintaining applications, or attending code reviews with the specific improvement they produced. Retain ordinary duties only when needed to establish a role requirement and supported by a concrete example.
- Usually select 3–5 strong bullets per role; adjust for relevance and space. Put the most compelling and relevant accomplishments first.
- Keep the summary short and specific. Keep technology lists compact; use job-description terms truthfully, without keyword stuffing.
- Include leadership, community work, volunteering, awards, or mentoring when supported and useful. Prefer an example over broad claims of leadership.
- Preserve the user's voice and accurate job titles. Flag a discrepancy between live records and requested wording instead of silently treating it as verified history.

## AI work

When relevant and supported, describe an AI feature or workflow that was delivered, the user's engineering ownership, how outputs were validated, and its outcome. Explain where ordinary code handles reliable calculations or rules and where the model supplies inference; use technical terms only where the audience benefits.

Claude Code, Codex CLI, or other development tools can be mentioned when the user wants them, but distinguish using tools to build software from shipping an AI product feature. A tool list alone does not demonstrate delivery. Do not fabricate evaluation results, guardrails, or improvements in speed or quality.

## Review and delivery

- Check each claim: could the user explain what happened, their contribution, and the basis for the result?
- Check the interview story: what should a stranger remember, and what questions should these bullets invite?
- Prioritize readable formatting over decoration. Keep the existing layout unless a redesign is requested or readability needs repair.
- For chat-only edits, provide the revised selected section as a reusable writing block. For PDF, Word, or Drive work, use the applicable available document skill and verify the actual output.
- If exporting role variants, use one approved content base and preserve the intended naming variants. Check PDF text and rendering for missing content, clipped text, broken links, or accidental extra resume sections.
- Report the changed sections and any unresolved evidence questions concisely. Prepare a reviewable draft before replacing a canonical resume or submitting LinkedIn changes, unless that exact write is already authorized. Review/draft requests do not authorize publishing, sharing, or archiving.
